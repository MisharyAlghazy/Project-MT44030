\section{Location of maximum shear stress}
\label{sec:a1-1}

The shear stresses follow from the Prandtl stress function:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{xy} = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z},
    \qquad
    \tau_{xz} = -\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}
    \label{eq:a1-tau}
\end{equation}
The shear stress is tangent to the contour lines of $\phi$ and its size is equal to the slope of $\phi$. The slope is large where many contour lines are close together. $\tau_{xy}$ depends on the slope in $z$-direction and $\tau_{xz}$ on the slope in $y$-direction.

In Figure~\ref{fig:a1-prandtl} the most contour lines (13) fit in the top plate, and there they run horizontally. So $\phi$ changes fast in $z$-direction, which gives the maximum $|\tau_{xy}|$ in the middle of the top plate, with $\tau_{xz}=0$. On the inclined walls the lines run steeply, so there $\phi$ changes mostly in $y$-direction. Most lines fit in these walls just below the $y$-axis (11), which gives the maximum $|\tau_{xz}|$ there, with opposite sign on the left and right wall. In the bottom only 5 to 7 lines fit in the wall, so the stress is lower there.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/a1_prandtl_marked}
    \caption{Contours of the Prandtl stress function of section A (Figure~4 of the assignment) with the locations of maximum $|\tau_{xy}|$ and $|\tau_{xz}|$ and the regions with the highest inaccuracy (dashed).}
    \label{fig:a1-prandtl}
\end{figure}

\finalanswer{$|\tau_{xy}|$ is maximal in the middle of the top plate. $|\tau_{xz}|$ is maximal on the two inclined walls just below the $y$-axis, with opposite sign on both sides.}

\section{Inaccuracies of the Prandtl-based prediction}
\label{sec:a1-2}

The inner boundary is free of stress, so for an exact solution $\phi$ would have to be constant along it, which means the inner boundary should follow one contour line. In Figure~\ref{fig:a1-prandtl} this is not the case. This gives two errors:
\begin{itemize}
    \item At the inner top corners and along the inner side of the inclined walls, contour lines end on the inner boundary. There the predicted shear stress is not tangent to the boundary, so part of it points through the free surface.
    \item The value of $\phi$ on the inner boundary is about $0.2$ at the top plate, but only about $0.1$ at the bottom. In a closed thin-walled section the shear flow should be constant around the section (Bredt), so the predicted stress at the bottom is too low.
\end{itemize}
The prediction is only good in the middle of the top plate, where the inner boundary follows the contour lines.

\finalanswer{The inner boundary is not a contour line of $\phi$, so the predicted stress is not tangent to the inner boundary and the shear flow is not constant. The largest errors are at the inner top corners and along the inner side of the inclined walls (dashed in Figure~\ref{fig:a1-prandtl}).}

\section{Cross-sectional efficiency}
\label{sec:a1-3}

The inner boundary is the outer boundary scaled by $k$. Areas scale with length$^2$ and torsional constants with length$^4$, so
\begin{equation}
    A_{inner} = k^2 A_{solid}, \qquad A_{hollow} = (1-k^2)\,A_{solid}, \qquad I_{p,inner} = k^4\,I_{p,solid}
\end{equation}
As for the hollow ellipse in the lectures, the torsional constant of the hole is subtracted from that of the solid section. This is only exact when the inner boundary is a contour line, which is not fully the case here (Question~\ref{sec:a1-2}), so
\begin{equation}
    I_{p,hollow} \approx I_{p,solid} - I_{p,inner} = (1-k^4)\,I_{p,solid}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \eta(k) = \frac{(I_p/A)_{hollow}}{(I_p/A)_{solid}}
    = \frac{1-k^4}{1-k^2}
    = \frac{(1-k^2)(1+k^2)}{1-k^2}
    = 1+k^2 .
\end{equation}

            \addplot[thick, red, dashed] {1 - x^4};
            \addlegendentry{$I_{p,hollow}/I_{p,solid} = 1-k^4$}
            \addplot[thick, black, dotted] {1 - x^2};
            \addlegendentry{$A_{hollow}/A_{solid} = 1-k^2$}
        \end{axis}
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \caption{Cross-sectional efficiency $\eta(k)$ and the relative stiffness and area of the hollow section.}
    \label{fig:efficiency}
\end{figure}

The efficiency goes up with $k$, from 1 for a solid section to 2 for a very thin wall, because the material near the centre carries little stress. But the stiffness goes down with $(1-k^4)$, and so does the strength: the maximum stress stays on the outer boundary and becomes $1/(1-k^4)$ times higher for the same torque, so $M_{t,max,hollow} = (1-k^4)\,M_{t,max,solid}$. At $k=0.8$ the section uses 36\,\% of the material with $\eta=1.64$, but carries only 59\,\% of the torque. Above $k\approx0.9$ the efficiency hardly increases, while the strength drops fast and thin walls can buckle. So $k$ should not be chosen too close to 1.

\finalanswer{$A_{inner}=k^2A_{solid}$, $A_{hollow}=(1-k^2)A_{solid}$, $I_{p,inner}=k^4I_{p,solid}$, $I_{p,hollow}\approx(1-k^4)I_{p,solid}$ and $\eta(k)=1+k^2$. The efficiency increases with $k$, but the stiffness and strength decrease with $(1-k^4)$.}
