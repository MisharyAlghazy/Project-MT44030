\section{Location of maximum shear stress}
\label{sec:a1-1}

\questionbox{Based on the displayed stress function, identify the location(s) of
maximum shear stress $\tau_{xy}$ and $\tau_{xz}$. Include your reasoning.
\textbf{(3 pts)}}

The shear stresses follow from the derivatives of the Prandtl stress function:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{xy} = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z},
    \qquad
    \tau_{xz} = -\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}
    \label{eq:a1-tau}
\end{equation}
So the shear stress is tangent to the contour lines of $\phi$, and its size is equal to the slope of $\phi$. In a contour plot the slope is large where the contour lines are close together. $\tau_{xy}$ only depends on the slope in $z$-direction and $\tau_{xz}$ only on the slope in $y$-direction.

In Figure~\ref{fig:a1-prandtl} we see the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item In the \emph{middle of the top plate}, around $(y,z)=(0,0.9a)$, almost all contour lines are squeezed into the thin top wall and they run horizontally. So $\phi$ changes fast in $z$-direction and not in $y$-direction. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:a1-tau} this gives the maximum $|\tau_{xy}|$, while $\tau_{xz}=0$ there.
    \item On the \emph{straight inclined side walls}, around $z=0$, the contour lines are also close together, but here they run steeply. So $\phi$ changes mostly in $y$-direction, which gives the maximum $|\tau_{xz}|$. Because the stress flows around the section, $\tau_{xz}$ has the opposite sign on the left and right wall.
\end{itemize}
At the bottom fewer contour lines fit in the wall, so the slope and the stress are lower there. In the outer top corners the contour lines bend away from the corner, so the stress goes to zero there.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/a1_prandtl_marked}
    \caption{Contours of the Prandtl stress function of section A (Figure~4 of the assignment) with the locations of maximum $|\tau_{xy}|$ and $|\tau_{xz}|$ (Question~\ref{sec:a1-1}) and the regions with the highest inaccuracy (dashed, Question~\ref{sec:a1-2}).}
    \label{fig:a1-prandtl}
\end{figure}

\finalanswer{$|\tau_{xy}|$ is maximal in the middle of the top plate, $(y,z)\approx(0,0.9a)$. $|\tau_{xz}|$ is maximal on the straight parts of the two inclined side walls, around $z=0$, with opposite sign on both sides.}


\section{Inaccuracies of the Prandtl-based prediction}
\label{sec:a1-2}

\questionbox{Considering the mismatch between the inner boundary and innermost
contour lines, which inaccuracies are present in the Prandtl-based shear stress
prediction? Mark regions with highest inaccuracy on the figure. \textbf{(3 pts)}}

For a hollow section the inner boundary must be free of stress, just like the outer boundary. In the Prandtl solution this means that $\phi$ has to be constant along the inner boundary, so the inner boundary should follow one contour line. In Figure~\ref{fig:a1-prandtl} this is not the case: the inner boundary cuts through several contour lines. This gives two inaccuracies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Where contour lines run into the inner boundary, the predicted shear stress is not tangent to the boundary. Part of it points through the free inner surface, which is not possible. This happens most at the inner top corners and on the lower part of the inclined walls, where they go into the rounded bottom (dashed in Figure~\ref{fig:a1-prandtl}).
    \item The value of $\phi$ on the inner boundary is not the same everywhere. Next to the side walls and the top plate it is around $0.2$, at the bottom only about $0.12$ (counting the contour lines in the wall). For a closed thin-walled section the shear flow ($\approx$ the difference in $\phi$ between the outer and inner boundary) should be constant around the section (Bredt). So the predicted stress at the bottom is too low compared to the rest of the section.
\end{itemize}
The prediction is best in the middle of the top plate and on the upper part of the inclined walls, where the inner boundary runs along the contour lines.

\finalanswer{The inner boundary is not a contour line of $\phi$. So the predicted stress is not tangent to the inner boundary, and the shear flow is not constant around the section (too low at the bottom). The largest errors are at the inner top corners and on the lower parts of the inclined walls near the bottom (dashed in Figure~\ref{fig:a1-prandtl}).}

\section{Cross-sectional efficiency}
\label{sec:a1-3}

\questionbox{Using $k$, express the areas $A_{inner}$ and $A_{hollow}$ in terms of
$A_{solid}$, and the torsional constants $I_{p,inner}$ and $I_{p,hollow}$ in terms
of $I_{p,solid}$. Obtain and plot the cross-sectional efficiency
$\eta(k)=(I_{p,hollow}/A_{hollow})/(I_{p,solid}/A_{solid})$. Discuss the balance
between the cross-sectional efficiency and strength. \textbf{(3 pts)}}

\paragraph{Areas.}
The inner boundary is the outer boundary scaled by $k$. Areas scale with length$^2$, so
\begin{equation}
    A_{inner} = k^2 A_{solid},
    \qquad
    A_{hollow} = A_{solid} - A_{inner} = (1-k^2)\,A_{solid}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Torsional constants.}
The torsional constant has the unit length$^4$ (e.g.\ $I_p = \frac{\pi}{2}r^4$ for a circle). The inner section is the solid section scaled by $k$, so
\begin{equation}
    I_{p,inner} = k^4\,I_{p,solid}
\end{equation}
In the lectures, the hollow ellipse was solved by subtracting the torsional constant of the hole from that of the solid section, $M_t = G\theta\,(I_{p,0} - I_{p,i})$. This is exact when the inner boundary is a contour line of $\phi$. As shown in Question~\ref{sec:a1-2}, that is not fully true for section A, so here it is an approximation:
\begin{equation}
    I_{p,hollow} \approx I_{p,solid} - I_{p,inner} = (1-k^4)\,I_{p,solid}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Cross-sectional efficiency.}
\begin{equation}
    \eta(k) = \frac{(I_p/A)_{hollow}}{(I_p/A)_{solid}}
    = \frac{1-k^4}{1-k^2}
    = \frac{(1-k^2)(1+k^2)}{1-k^2}
    = 1+k^2
\end{equation}
This is the same result as for the hollow circle in the lectures. It is plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:efficiency}.

\paragraph{Efficiency versus strength.}
The efficiency goes up with $k$, from 1 for the solid section to 2 for a very thin wall. Taking material away from the core is efficient, because the core only carries a small part of the stress. But the absolute stiffness goes down with $(1-k^4)$, and so does the strength: the maximum shear stress stays on the outer boundary and for the same torque it becomes a factor $1/(1-k^4)$ higher (as for the hollow ellipse in the lectures). So
\begin{equation}
    M_{t,max,hollow} = (1-k^4)\,M_{t,max,solid}
\end{equation}
For example, at $k=0.8$ the section uses 36\,\% of the material and $\eta=1.64$, but it can only carry 59\,\% of the torque of the solid section. Above $k\approx0.9$ the efficiency hardly increases anymore, while the strength drops quickly. Very thin walls can also buckle, and the Prandtl approximation gets worse (Question~\ref{sec:a1-2}). So a moderate $k$ gives the best balance between saving material and keeping enough strength.

\finalanswer{$A_{inner}=k^2A_{solid}$, $A_{hollow}=(1-k^2)A_{solid}$, $I_{p,inner}=k^4I_{p,solid}$, $I_{p,hollow}\approx(1-k^4)I_{p,solid}$ and $\eta(k)=1+k^2$. The efficiency increases with $k$, but the stiffness and strength decrease with $(1-k^4)$.}
