\section{Location of maximum shear stress}
\label{sec:a1-1}
The shear stresses follow from the derivatives of the Prandtl stress function:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{xy} = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z},
    \qquad
    \tau_{xz} = -\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}
    \label{eq:a1-tau}
\end{equation}
So the shear stress is always tangent to the contour lines of $\phi$, and its size is equal to the slope of $\phi$. In a contour plot the slope is large where the contour lines are close together. Each stress component only depends on the slope in one direction: $\tau_{xy}$ on the slope in $z$-direction and $\tau_{xz}$ on the slope in $y$-direction.

In Figure~\ref{fig:a1-prandtl} we see the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item At the \emph{middle of the top plate}, around $(y,z)=(0,a)$, all contour lines are squeezed into the thin top wall and they run horizontally. So $\phi$ changes fast in $z$-direction and not at all in $y$-direction. From Eq.~\eqref{eq:a1-tau} this means that $\tau_{xy}$ is maximal here and $\tau_{xz}=0$.
    \item On the \emph{inclined side walls}, just below the $y$-axis, the contour lines are also close together, but here they run steeply. So $\phi$ now changes mostly in $y$-direction, which gives the maximum $\tau_{xz}$. Because the flow goes around the section, $\tau_{xz}$ has opposite signs on the left and right wall.
\end{itemize}
At the bottom of the section the contour lines are far apart, so the stress is low there. In the outer top corners the contour lines bend away from the corner, so the stress goes to zero there.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/A1_prandtl (2).jpeg}
    \caption{Contours of the Prandtl stress function in the wall of section A (based on Figure~4 of the assignment, $k=0.8$). Marked are the locations of maximum $|\tau_{xy}|$ and $|\tau_{xz}|$ (Question~\ref{sec:a1-1}) and the regions with the highest inaccuracy (dashed, Question~\ref{sec:a1-2}).}
    \label{fig:a1-prandtl}
\end{figure}

\finalanswer{$|\tau_{xy}|$ is maximal at the middle of the top plate, $(y,z)=(0,a)$. $|\tau_{xz}|$ is maximal on the two inclined side walls just below the $y$-axis (on both sides, with opposite sign).}


\section{Inaccuracies of the Prandtl-based prediction}
\label{sec:a1-2}

The stress function we used was derived for a solid section. For a hollow section it would only be exact if the inner boundary followed one of the contour lines of $\phi$, because then $\phi$ is constant along it. In Figure~\ref{fig:a1-prandtl} we can see that this is not really the case: the inner boundary crosses several contour lines. This is worst near the inner top corners (dashed in the figure), and to a smaller extent on the upper part of the inclined walls.

This causes two problems. First, the shear stress on the inner wall is no longer tangent to the boundary, so part of it would point through the inner surface. That cannot happen, since nothing acts on that surface. Second, the magnitude is off. In a thin-walled closed section we would expect a roughly constant shear flow around the section, but this solution gives a stress that varies quite a lot along the wall.

The prediction works best at the centre of the top wall and at the bottom, since there the inner boundary runs almost parallel to the contour lines.

\finalanswer{The largest error is at the inner top corners (dashed in Figure~\ref{fig:a1-prandtl}), where the predicted shear stress is not tangent to the inner wall and the shear flow is not constant. The prediction is most accurate at the centre of the top wall and at the bottom.}

\section{Cross-sectional efficiency}
\label{sec:a1-3}

\paragraph{Areas.}
The inner boundary is the outer boundary scaled by $k$. Areas scale with length$^2$, so
\begin{equation}
    A_{inner} = k^2 A_{solid},
    \qquad
    A_{hollow} = A_{solid} - A_{inner} = (1-k^2)\,A_{solid}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Torsional constants.}
The torsional constant has the unit length$^4$ (e.g.\ $I_p = \frac{\pi}{2}r^4$ for a circle). Since the inner section is the solid section scaled by $k$:
\begin{equation}
    I_{p,inner} = k^4\,I_{p,solid}
\end{equation}
In the lectures, the hollow ellipse was solved by subtracting the torsional constant of the hole from that of the solid section, $M_t = G\theta\,(I_{p,0} - I_{p,i})$. This is exact when the inner boundary lies on a contour line of $\phi$. As shown in Question~\ref{sec:a1-2}, that is not fully true for section A, so here it is an approximation:
\begin{equation}
    I_{p,hollow} \approx I_{p,solid} - I_{p,inner} = (1-k^4)\,I_{p,solid}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Cross-sectional efficiency.}
\begin{equation}
    \eta(k) = \frac{(I_p/A)_{hollow}}{(I_p/A)_{solid}}
    = \frac{1-k^4}{1-k^2}
    = \frac{(1-k^2)(1+k^2)}{1-k^2}
    = 1+k^2
\end{equation}
This is the same result as for the hollow circle in the lectures. It is plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:efficiency}.

\paragraph{Efficiency versus strength.}
The efficiency increases with $k$, from 1 for the solid section to 2 for a very thin wall. Taking material away from the core is efficient, because the core only carries a small part of the stress. But the absolute stiffness goes down with $(1-k^4)$. The strength goes down in the same way: the maximum shear stress stays on the outer boundary, and for the same torque it becomes a factor $1/(1-k^4)$ higher (compare $\phi \propto M_t/(1-k^4)$ for the hollow ellipse in the lectures). So
\begin{equation}
    M_{t,max,hollow} = (1-k^4)\,M_{t,max,solid}
\end{equation}
For example, at $k=0.8$ the section uses 36\% of the material and $\eta=1.64$, but it only carries 59\% of the torque of the solid section. Above $k\approx0.9$ the efficiency hardly increases anymore, while the strength drops quickly. Very thin walls can also buckle in shear, and the Prandtl approximation becomes worse (Question~\ref{sec:a1-2}). So a k should not be chosen close to 1

\finalanswer{$A_{inner}=k^2A_{solid}$, $A_{hollow}=(1-k^2)A_{solid}$, $I_{p,inner}=k^4I_{p,solid}$, $I_{p,hollow}\approx(1-k^4)I_{p,solid}$ and $\eta(k)=1+k^2$. The efficiency increases with $k$, but the stiffness and strength decrease with $(1-k^4)$.}
